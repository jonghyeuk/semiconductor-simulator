# CLEANROOM INK — a visual philosophy

**Philosophy:** The dignity of the night shift, drawn in ink. Meaning carried by posture, tone, and a single lit word.

---

Cleanroom Ink is a black-and-paper discipline. The world is rendered in one confident ink weight on warm, slightly aged paper — the color of a manual left on a workbench for ten years. Nothing is shaded for prettiness; every mark earns its place. Where a lesser hand would draw a face, this one draws a hood, goggles, a breath-fogged mask — the anonymized figure of the fab, where craft shows in the *stance* of a body, not the features of a face. The restraint is the point. The figures are iconic to the edge of a pictogram yet unmistakably human, and that tension is where the work reads as labored-over rather than generated.

Space breathes through screentone — the halftone dot, the oldest honest texture in comics, laid down in patient fields to suggest the cold fluorescent hum of a subfab without ever illustrating it literally. Dots gather at the floor and the far wall; they thin to bare paper where the eye must rest. Equipment is present as silhouette and cross-section line: a mainframe's shoulder, a chamber's cylinder, the ribbon of a gas line. These are quoted, not detailed — the fab is felt at the periphery so the human moment holds the center.

Color is rationed to a single spot, and it is never decoration — it is *information given a body*. One cyan, or one amber, reserved exclusively for the English that matters: the word on the HMI, the alarm on the banner, the line of dialogue a person must actually read to do their job safely. Everything else recedes to ink. The reader's eye is trained, panel after panel, that the lit word is the one to learn. This is the whole pedagogy hidden inside the aesthetic: attention is drawn by contrast, not by instruction.

Composition follows the grammar of the comics page — the bold outer frame, the decisive gutter, the speech balloon whose tail *points*, giving each utterance an owner. Balloons are clean-edged and generous; the English sits large and calm, a whispered Korean gloss beneath it like a subtitle, never competing. Motion, when a moment demands it — an alarm, a hand thrown up — arrives as a few straight speed-lines and a hard flash of the spot color, and then the page goes quiet again. Drama is spent, not sprayed.

Every panel must look like the frame of a story someone believed in: the linework closed and certain, the screentone even and deliberate, the single color placed with a jeweler's patience. Master-level execution here means knowing what to leave as bare paper. The finished set should feel like pages torn from a well-loved industrial manga — meticulously inked, painstakingly composed, the product of a hand at the top of its craft — that happen, quietly, to teach a person the exact English they will meet on their first shift.
